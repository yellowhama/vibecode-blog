> **TL;DR**: An excavation of can ai have "wisdom"?. Real scars, no slop.

## TL;DR

- Running local AI got me into hardware news. That led me to search for IBM for the first time in my life.
- Found a **DIKW Pyramid** lecture on IBM's YouTube. Data, Information, Knowledge, Wisdom. A framework for where AI actually stands.
- The point: AI is incredible at **Data, Information, and Knowledge**. But **Wisdom**? Not yet.
- The deal: let AI handle the "How." Keep the "Why" and the "What" for yourself.

---

## Local AI Gives You Strange Interests

I started running local LLMs and picked up a weird habit.

Checking hardware news.

"NVIDIA dropped a new GPU." "AMD announced something." "Apple M4 whatever..."

VRAM matters when you run Boksuni. Scraping by on 16GB, you start thinking "32GB and I could run two models at once." And before you know it, you're following hardware news.

Then a random thought hit me.

**"What's IBM doing these days?"**

I'm pretty sure it was the first time in my life I searched for IBM. Maybe ten years? No — literally never. I just vaguely knew them as "the company that used to make computers."

"Do they still make things?"

---

## IBM Has a YouTube Channel

Found IBM's official YouTube channel while searching.

Expected product ads. Corporate PR videos. Something like that.

Nope.

**Lectures.**

A guy who clearly looks like he's good with computers comes out and explains things seriously.

Drawing on a blackboard.

One title caught my eye.

**"The Limits of AI"**

The limits of AI? Interesting.

Twenty-minute video. Watched the whole thing. This guy was a great lecturer. I thought he was on the level of Jeong Seung-je (a famous Korean tutor), maybe even better.

His title was this:

**Distinguished Engineer (IBM)**

To explain:

- One of IBM's highest technical ranks
- Above Senior, above Principal Engineer
- A level that influences company-wide technical direction and architecture
- Not so much a researcher — more of a hands-on engineer + technical thinker + internal opinion leader

Put simply:

> "Practically the top position you can reach at IBM through technical skill."

For reference, IBM's tech ladder looks like this:

- Engineer
- Senior Engineer
- **Distinguished Engineer**
- IBM Fellow (basically legendary)

So when this person says "AI has gotten this far, and it's stuck here" — it's not just a personal opinion.

It's the summary of someone who's spent decades actually building and breaking systems inside IBM.

And it was impressive enough that I'm writing it up here.

---

## "AI Will Never Do That" — Things People Got Wrong

The lecture opened like this:

> "For decades, people said with confidence: 'AI will never be able to do this.' And you know what those predictions have in common? Most of them were wrong."

Then he listed the examples.

**1997**: "A computer beating a chess grandmaster is impossible."

IBM Deep Blue beat Kasparov.

**2011**: "Understanding the nuance of language, wordplay, humor — AI can't do that."

IBM Watson won Jeopardy! (A quiz show built on puns and wordplay.)

**2016**: "Go has 10^170 possible positions. Give it a hundred years."

AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol.

**2020**: "Creative writing and art are uniquely human."

GPT-3 arrived. Now AI draws pictures and composes music.

See the pattern?

The lecturer wrapped it up like this:

> "My advice is this: don't bet on the limitations of AI. Not if you don't want to be wrong."

---

## But Real Limits Do Exist

That said, he wasn't calling AI omnipotent.

He drew a pyramid on the blackboard.

Called it the **DIKW Pyramid**. Data-Information-Knowledge-Wisdom.

"To understand where AI has reached and where it's stuck, you need to know this framework."

---

## Level 1: Data — Just a Pile of Numbers

He wrote some numbers.

`10, 6, 42, 8`

"This is data. Looking at just this, you have no idea what it means. Could be phone numbers, temperatures, or completely meaningless digits."

**Data = raw facts. No context.**

Stuff sitting in databases. Meaningless by itself.

---

## Level 2: Information — Data With Context

"Now let's add context. These numbers are the ages of people in a room."

`Age 10, age 6, age 42, age 8`

Oh. Now something clicks. Three kids and one adult in the room.

**Information = data with context.**

That's what "Information Technology" means. Making data useful by giving it context.

---

## Level 3: Knowledge — Interpreted Information

"Interpret the information and you get knowledge."

`"The majority of people in this room are under 21."`

Not just knowing the ages — **discovering a pattern**. Three out of four are minors.

**Knowledge = interpretation + pattern discovery.**

He emphasized this part:

> "This is exactly where AI shines. Finding patterns in massive amounts of information and providing meaningful analysis. AI is phenomenally good at this."

---

## Level 4: Wisdom — Applied Knowledge

"Last one. **Apply** knowledge to a real situation, and you get wisdom."

`"Most of them are kids with one adult, so let's prepare age-appropriate activities. The 42-year-old can probably join in for a bit too."`

It's not just knowing "most are under 21." It's **deciding what to do about it**.

**Wisdom = application + judgment + responsibility.**

He stopped here.

> "And this is exactly the area AI hasn't reached yet."

---

## Why Is Wisdom Hard for AI?

The lecture explained several reasons.

### 1. There's No Right Answer

"What should happen in this room" **depends on the situation**.

- Birthday party? Play games.
- School class? Educational activities.
- Evacuation? Get out fast.

Not "right or wrong." It's a question of **what you prioritize**.

### 2. Context Is Infinite

Making a wise decision means considering too many things.

- The relationships between these people?
- How much time is there?
- The budget?
- The 42-year-old's personality?
- What the kids like?

Hard to compress all that context into a single answer.

### 3. Responsibility Follows

Wisdom is a **decision**, and decisions carry **responsibility**.

If AI says "do this" and it goes wrong? Who's responsible?

> "A system producing an answer and a system bearing responsibility for that answer are completely different problems."

### 4. Some Things Can't Be Put Into Words

Experienced people sometimes judge by "just a feeling."

Decades of experience compressed into **tacit knowledge**. They can't explain why themselves, but their judgment is right more often than not.

This kind of thing is nearly impossible to train from data.

---

## So What Are the Roles of Humans and AI?

The final part of the lecture was memorable.

He split the whiteboard in half.

**Left: Human Territory**

- **WHAT** — What are we doing? (Goals)
- **WHY** — Why are we doing it? (Purpose, meaning)

**Right: AI Territory**

- **HOW** — How do we do it? (Execution, optimization)

> "Humans set the 'what' and 'why.' AI handles the 'how.' Agentic AI can automate a great deal, but you still have to tell it what to do in the first place."

For example:

**Human**: "I want to improve customer satisfaction." (WHAT + WHY)
**AI**: "Analyzed customer feedback. Top 10 complaints identified. Improvement proposals ready." (HOW)

**Human**: "Find me good stocks." (WHAT)
**AI**: "Analyzed 586 stocks by P/E and ROE. Top 30 listed." (HOW)

But **"Should I buy this stock?"** — AI can't answer that. That's the domain of wisdom, a judgment that carries responsibility.

---

## What He Said at the End

The lecturer wrapped up like this:

> "Looking at AI's history, it seems like almost nothing happens for a very long time, and then it suddenly explodes. We're at that inflection point right now."

> "There are still unsolved problems. AGI, self-awareness, genuine understanding, wisdom... But what's interesting is that these problems still remain. If you like solving problems, that means there's plenty of work left."

> "One more time: don't bet on the limitations of AI. The things people swore 'would never happen' keep getting broken."

---

## My Take

Glad I stumbled into IBM's YouTube.

Honestly, I had no idea IBM was making educational content like this. For a corporate channel, there was no ad smell — it genuinely felt like they were trying to **teach**.

The DIKW Pyramid seems like a pretty useful framework.

When I tell Boksuni "find me good content," Boksuni nails it. Scrapes data, organizes information, finds patterns, scores everything.

But when I ask "is this article actually good?" — it gets fuzzy. A high score doesn't mean it's actually good. In the end, I have to read it myself and decide.

**That's the domain of wisdom.**

AI handles DIK (Data-Information-Knowledge), so I can focus on W (Wisdom). Division of labor.

Don't ask Claude "should I do this or not?"
Ask it "analyze this for me." AI isn't an advisor. It's an analysis engine.

**I make the call. I take the responsibility.**

I think that's how you work with AI.
